# 2.X Methylation Data Acquisition, Quality Control, and Probe Filtering

## 2.X.1 Data source and acquisition

DNA methylation data for this chapter were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) series GSE42861, a whole-blood Illumina HumanMethylation450 (450K) BeadChip dataset generated by Liu et al. (2013) as part of an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) investigating DNA methylation as an intermediary of genetic risk in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Direct interrogation of the GEO record (accessed 2026-08-01) confirmed that the series comprises 689 samples profiled on platform GPL13534 (Illumina HumanMethylation450 BeadChip), and that GEO hosts the data in two forms: (i) raw per-sample IDAT scan files (`GSE42861_RAW.tar`, approximately 5.7 GB compressed), and (ii) processed/normalized data, including a GEO series matrix file that embeds author-normalized beta values directly alongside the sample metadata header.

This chapter uses the processed series matrix (`GSE42861_series_matrix.txt.gz`, approximately 2.5 GB compressed), retrieved programmatically from the NCBI GEO FTP mirror (`https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/series/GSE42nnn/GSE42861/matrix/GSE42861_series_matrix.txt.gz`) rather than the raw IDAT archive. This decision was made for two compounding reasons. First, and most decisively, converting 689 raw IDAT files into normalized beta values requires the `minfi` Bioconductor framework (Aryee et al., 2014), which loads per-probe red/green channel intensities for every sample simultaneously; for a 450K array (approximately 485,000 probes) across 689 samples, this intermediate representation substantially exceeds the working memory of the 16 GB machine used for this analysis once duplicate copies are held during normalization, background correction, and dye-bias adjustment. Reprocessing from IDATs was therefore not a computationally tractable option on the available hardware without additional infrastructure (e.g. cloud compute or chunked/out-of-core processing), which was outside the scope of this chapter. Second, using the GEO-deposited, author-normalized values preserves direct comparability with the originally published analysis (Liu et al., 2013), since the beta values analyzed here are numerically identical to those the original authors generated and validated, rather than being the output of an independently re-implemented normalization pipeline that could introduce systematic differences from the published EWAS.

The trade-off inherent in this choice is explicit and is compensated for methodologically rather than ignored: using processed beta values forfeits access to raw fluorescence intensities, and therefore to intensity-based per-probe detection p-values, which are one standard means of flagging technically unreliable probes at the single-array level (Teschendorff & Relton, 2018). This chapter substitutes a probe-level filtering cascade based on independently derived, sequence- and mapping-based probe quality annotations (Section 2.X.6), which identifies a materially overlapping set of unreliable probes (cross-reactive, polymorphic, and multi-mapping probes) through a different, complementary route that does not depend on having raw intensity data.

Using an already-normalized series matrix does not make the sample-level QC (Section 2.X.5) or probe-level filtering (Section 2.X.6) applied later in this pipeline redundant: normalization and quality control operate on different axes of data quality and address different failure modes. Normalization, here performed once by Liu et al. (2013) upstream of GEO deposition, is a signal-processing step, correcting systematic technical effects shared across an array (dye bias, background fluorescence, between-sample scaling) so that beta values are comparable across samples. It says nothing about whether a given array or a given probe is individually trustworthy. A sample can be fully and correctly normalized and still be a technical outlier, for instance from poor bisulfite conversion or a sex/label mix-up, exactly the failure modes the PCA-outlier and chrY sex checks in Section 2.X.5 are designed to catch. Likewise, a probe can be fully and correctly normalized and still be scientifically unusable, for instance because its sequence cross-hybridizes to multiple genomic loci or overlaps a common SNP, which normalization has no mechanism to detect or correct, and which the sequence- and mapping-based filters in Section 2.X.6 target instead. Consequently, sample-level QC and probe-level filtering are standard, expected steps even on pre-normalized, publicly deposited datasets (Teschendorff & Relton, 2018), and are applied here for that reason rather than as a duplication of the normalization already performed.

## 2.X.2 Software environment

Data acquisition and processing were implemented in R. Three packages were used for reasons specific to the scale of this dataset rather than general convenience:

- `data.table` (Dowle & Srinivasan, 2023) provided the `fread()` function used to stream-parse the multi-gigabyte, tab-delimited series matrix file. Base R's `read.table`/`read.csv` family parses text row-by-row with substantial per-cell overhead and was not practical at this file size on the available hardware; `fread` uses a compiled, memory-mapped parser that scales to files of this size directly from a shell pipe (see Section 2.X.4).
- `matrixStats` (Bengtsson, 2023) provided `rowVars()`, a vectorized, compiled implementation of per-row variance used to rank approximately 485,000 probes by cross-sample variance (Section 2.X.5). A base R equivalent (`apply(beta, 1, var)`) would require looping in interpreted R across every probe and was avoided for the same tractability reason.
- `ChAMP` and its companion data package `ChAMPdata` (Morris et al., 2014; Tian et al., 2017) were installed for their bundled reference resources rather than their pipeline functions: the Zhou et al. (2017) `MASK_general` probe-quality annotation, the `multi.hit` cross-mapping probe list, and the `probe.features` table (which supplies a per-probe chromosome assignment used both to build a chrY probe lookup, Section 2.X.5, and to remove sex-chromosome probes, Section 2.X.6). Installing the full `ChAMP` package is a heavier dependency than strictly required for these three data objects, but it is the standard, citable distribution channel for them and avoids maintaining a hand-curated copy of annotation resources whose provenance would otherwise need independent justification.

A dedicated setup script separates this one-time environment preparation from the analysis itself: it installs the packages above if absent, downloads the series matrix if not already present locally, and constructs a full probe-to-chromosome lookup table (`anno450k_full.rds`) directly from `ChAMPdata::probe.features` (chromosome values re-prefixed to the `chrN`/`chrX`/`chrY` convention used throughout downstream code). Separating acquisition from analysis in this way follows standard reproducible-pipeline practice: the expensive, one-time, network- and installation-dependent steps are isolated from the deterministic, re-runnable analysis steps, so that the analysis script can be re-executed repeatedly (e.g. during method development) without repeating a multi-gigabyte download each time.

## 2.X.3 Phenotype extraction

GEO series matrix files store two logically distinct blocks in one text file: a header block of sample-level metadata (each field as one `!Sample_characteristics_ch1` line, tab-separated across samples) followed by the numeric data matrix itself. Only the first 70 lines of the file, comprising the complete header block, were read for phenotype extraction, rather than the full file. This is a direct consequence of the memory-conscious design established in Section 2.X.1: reading the header in isolation allows the cohort structure (sample counts, group balance, sex balance) to be verified before the much larger data block is loaded, so that any unexpected cohort composition is caught before the more memory-intensive parsing step is reached, rather than after.

Sample identifiers were read from the `!Sample_geo_accession` line. A key-matching function located, for each phenotype field of interest (disease state, a field labelled "subject", age, sex, smoking status), the corresponding `!Sample_characteristics_ch1` line by testing which line's values begin with that field's key text, then extracted and trimmed the value for every sample from that line. This approach is necessary rather than incidental: GEO does not guarantee a fixed line order or a fixed total number of `!Sample_characteristics_ch1` lines across different series, so a key-matching approach is more robust to the specific header layout of GSE42861 than a fixed-line-number assumption would be. Direct inspection of the live GEO file confirmed the observed value of the "subject" field is a categorical status label (e.g. "Patient"), not a unique per-individual identifier; it is retained in the phenotype table as extracted, without further interpretation, and should not be treated as a subject ID for repeated-measures or pairing purposes.

A binary disease-status variable was derived by testing the free-text disease-state field for the substring "rheumatoid" (case-insensitive), yielding an explicit two-level RA-versus-Control grouping variable suitable for direct use in downstream statistical comparisons. Sample counts by group and by sex were tabulated and logged immediately, providing a human-checkable confirmation that the extracted cohort matches the expected composition of GSE42861 (689 samples split between RA cases and controls; Liu et al., 2013) before any further processing. A hard assertion additionally required every extracted sex value to be exactly "M" or "F"; this exists because the chrY-based sex-resolution procedure described in Section 2.X.5 assumes exactly two sex categories, and an unexpected or missing sex code would otherwise let that later logic silently mis-assign inferred sex rather than failing visibly at the point of the actual data problem.

## 2.X.4 Beta-value matrix construction

The beta-value matrix was parsed from the same series matrix file using `data.table::fread`, invoked on a `gzcat` shell pipe with reading anchored to begin at the row containing the `"ID_REF"` column header, so that the metadata block already consumed in Section 2.X.3 is skipped without being re-parsed as numeric data. Any trailing row whose first column begins with `!` (the GEO convention for structural tag rows, of which the `series_matrix_table_end` tag is the relevant instance at this position in the file) was removed, since such rows are not numeric methylation data and would otherwise be misread as an additional, spurious probe. The first column, containing CpG probe identifiers, was renamed and used to set row names on a purely numeric matrix; the remaining columns, one per GEO sample accession, were coerced to numeric and retained as column names.

Before proceeding, the column order of the parsed beta matrix was checked against the sample order of the phenotype table constructed independently in Section 2.X.3, using a hard assertion that halts execution on any mismatch. This check exists because the phenotype table and the beta matrix are parsed from the same file by two independent passes, and nothing in that process structurally guarantees that GEO sample order is preserved identically across both parses. Were this check omitted and a silent reordering to occur, each sample's methylation profile would be paired with the wrong phenotype record; critically, this class of error produces no warning or exception at the point it occurs and would instead surface only as biologically implausible or misleading downstream results (e.g. spurious or absent case-control differences), potentially far downstream of the actual defect. An explicit, fail-fast assertion at the point of construction is therefore a deliberate and load-bearing safeguard, not a defensive-programming formality.

Immediately after construction, the beta matrix was asserted to be strictly numeric. This guards against a specific silent-failure mode: R's `as.matrix()` applied to a `data.table` in which even a single value column failed to parse as numeric during import would silently coerce the *entire* matrix to character type rather than raising an error at that point, and the resulting problem would then surface only much later, at whichever downstream arithmetic step (e.g. the variance computation in Section 2.X.5) first required numeric input. An explicit type check immediately after construction converts this into an immediate, diagnosable failure at its true source instead.

Following matrix construction, the intermediate `data.table` object was removed and garbage collection was triggered explicitly. Given that the retained beta matrix alone (approximately 485,000 probes by 689 samples of double-precision values) is a substantial fraction of available memory on a 16 GB machine, retaining a duplicate `data.table` representation of the same data simultaneously would materially increase peak memory use for no further benefit once the matrix has been extracted.

## 2.X.5 Sample-level quality control

Sample-level quality control was performed to identify technically unreliable arrays and to verify the accuracy of self-reported sex, consistent with recommended statistical practice for DNA methylation array analysis (Teschendorff & Relton, 2018).

**Missingness.** The number of missing (NA) beta values per sample was computed across the full retained probe set, giving a first, simple indicator of per-array data completeness independent of any downstream statistical model.

**Principal component analysis and outlier detection.** Principal component analysis (PCA) was restricted to a reduced probe subset selected in two steps, rather than computed on the full probe set. First, probes with missing values in more than 5% of samples, the same threshold used in the probe-filtering step of Section 2.X.6, were excluded from consideration entirely. Second, PCA was restricted to the 20,000 probes with the highest cross-sample variance *among that reasonably complete subset*. Both steps serve distinct purposes: the initial completeness filter prevents a probe's apparent high variance from being an artefact of it having very few non-missing observations (a probe measured reliably in only a handful of samples can show spuriously extreme sample-to-sample variance that reflects small effective sample size rather than genuine biological signal), while the subsequent variance ranking is a deliberate dimensionality reduction, since probes with minimal variance across the cohort contribute little to sample-level separation on the leading components while adding proportionally to computational and memory cost. Missing values remaining within this final probe subset were imputed by the row-wise (probe) mean, solely to satisfy PCA's requirement for a complete input matrix; this imputation is local to the QC computation and does not affect the beta values retained in the final output matrix.

Outlier detection was performed on the resulting first two principal components (PC1, PC2), computed **separately within each self-reported sex group** rather than pooled across the full cohort. This design choice follows directly from the well-established observation that whole-blood (and most tissue) methylation array data separate strongly by sex on early principal components, driven by the biologically real difference in X-chromosome dosage and Y-chromosome presence/absence between male and female samples (Teschendorff & Relton, 2018). Performing outlier detection within sex strata prevents this expected, biologically meaningful between-sex separation on PC1 from being misidentified as a technical anomaly; without sex stratification, a purely technical MAD-based rule would risk flagging one entire sex as "outliers" relative to the other, which would be a methodological error rather than a genuine quality signal.

A sample was flagged as an outlier if it deviated by more than five median absolute deviations (MAD) from the median of either PC1 or PC2 within its sex stratum, following the robust outlier-detection framework of Leys et al. (2013). Leys et al. (2013) demonstrate that outlier detection based on the mean and standard deviation is not robust to the very outliers it is intended to detect, because a small number of extreme values inflate the standard deviation itself and can thereby mask their own detection; the median and the median absolute deviation are both computed from order statistics that are far less sensitive to extreme values, making the MAD-based rule appropriate specifically because sample-level PCA outliers are, by construction, the extreme values the procedure must remain sensitive to.

**Sex verification.** Self-reported sex was cross-checked against a methylation-derived biological signal: mean beta-value methylation across probes mapping to chromosome Y (chrY), using the chromosome lookup table constructed in Section 2.X.2. Two summaries of this chrY signal were computed: a simple median split, and unsupervised k-means clustering with two centers, run with 25 random starts and the best-fitting solution retained, since a single random initialization (k-means' default) is not guaranteed to avoid a poor local optimum. Critically, the direction of this signal, that is, whether male or female samples exhibit the higher mean chrY beta value, was **not assumed a priori**. This is a deliberate design decision: whether chrY probes read as more or less methylated in male versus female samples on a given array platform and dataset is an empirical property of that assay and tissue, and is not safe to assume as a fixed convention. Accordingly, the two k-means clusters were mapped to sex labels empirically, by assigning each cluster the reported sex that is most common (by majority) among the samples it contains, rather than by an assumed high-signal-means-male (or vice versa) rule. Each sample's chrY-cluster-derived sex was then compared to its self-reported sex, and any disagreement was flagged as a sex mismatch. This mismatch flag, together with the underlying PCA coordinates, per-sample missingness, PCA outlier status, and chrY mean signal, was merged into the phenotype table, and the full cross-tabulation of reported sex against chrY-inferred sex, along with a count of flagged mismatches, was logged for manual review. A discordance identified by this check can indicate sample or label swaps, which is a recognized and important class of technical error in array-based cohort studies that PCA-based outlier detection alone does not reliably catch, since a sex-mislabeled sample can otherwise appear to be a well-behaved member of the wrong sex-stratified PCA cluster.

Two diagnostic figures were produced. A PCA scatterplot displays each sample's position on PC1 and PC2 in two panels, one colored by disease group and one colored by self-reported sex, with the MAD-based outlier flag marked by a distinct plotting symbol in **both** panels, so that outlier status can be visually cross-referenced against either grouping variable. A beta-value density plot overlays the density curve of the top-variance probe subset for the first sample together with a random subsample of up to 150 additional sample profiles (rather than the full cohort, to keep the figure legible and to bound plotting cost independent of total sample count), colored by disease group, allowing visual identification of any sample whose overall methylation distribution deviates markedly from the rest of the cohort, a pattern consistent with a technical failure (e.g. poor bisulfite conversion or hybridization) that would not necessarily manifest as a PCA outlier. Both figures, and every subsequent figure produced in this chapter, are rendered at one fixed canvas size (2400 x 1800 px at 300 dpi) with no title embedded in the plot itself; figure captions are composed separately as part of the thesis text rather than rendered onto the image.

## 2.X.6 Probe-level filtering

Following sample-level QC, probe-level filtering was applied using a ChAMP-style Infinium probe quality-control cascade (Tian et al., 2017; Morris et al., 2014), drawing on the sequence- and mapping-based probe annotation of Zhou, Laird and Shen (2017) and on the earlier characterization of cross-reactive and polymorphic 450K probes by Chen et al. (2013). The filtering was applied as five sequential, logged criteria, so that the contribution of each individual criterion to the final retained probe count is explicit and independently auditable, rather than being collapsed into a single opaque filtering step.

1. **Probe type restriction.** Only probe identifiers with the `cg` prefix were retained. Identifiers with the `rs` prefix are SNP-genotyping control probes included on the array for sample-identity quality control unrelated to CpG methylation, and identifiers with the `ch` prefix measure non-CpG cytosine contexts; neither measures standard CpG methylation and both are conventionally excluded from CpG-level analyses.
2. **`MASK_general` removal.** Probes flagged in the Zhou et al. (2017) `MASK_general` annotation were removed. This annotation was constructed by realigning probe sequences against the reference genome and consolidates several independent sources of technical unreliability, cross-reactivity to off-target genomic loci, overlap with common single-nucleotide polymorphisms at the probe-binding or single-base-extension position, and ambiguous or low-confidence mapping, into one combined exclusion list, making it a more comprehensive filter than any single one of these criteria applied in isolation.
3. **Multi-hit removal.** Probes identified as mapping to more than one genomic location (the `multi.hit` resource distributed with `ChAMPdata`) were excluded, since a probe that binds at multiple genomic locations cannot yield a methylation signal unambiguously attributable to a single CpG site, independent of whether that probe also happens to be flagged by `MASK_general`.
4. **Sex-chromosome removal.** Probes located on chromosome X or chromosome Y were excluded from the pooled analysis probe set. This directly addresses the same biological reality noted in Section 2.X.5: methylation on the sex chromosomes differs systematically between male and female samples for reasons unrelated to disease status (X-chromosome inactivation and Y-chromosome hemizygosity), so retaining these probes in a pooled case-control comparison would risk introducing sex-driven signal into an analysis intended to isolate disease-associated methylation differences, a confound that within-sex or sex-interaction analyses would need to handle differently but that a pooled analysis should avoid by exclusion.
5. **Missingness threshold.** Among probes surviving the first four filters, any probe with a missing value in more than 5% of samples was excluded, on the basis that a probe missing in a substantial fraction of the cohort provides an unreliable estimate of that CpG's methylation across the full sample set regardless of its sequence-level annotation status.

After filtering, the beta matrix was subset to the final retained probe list, and any residual missing values, expected to be sparse given the preceding 5% missingness threshold, were imputed by the row-wise (probe) mean across retained samples, with the number of imputed cells logged explicitly so that the extent of this final imputation step is auditable rather than silent. The probe annotation table was subset and keyed to match the final retained probe list exactly, ensuring the saved annotation corresponds one-to-one with the probes present in the final beta matrix.

## 2.X.7 Data export

The quality-controlled, filtered beta-value matrix, the phenotype table (incorporating the sample-level QC metrics derived in Section 2.X.5, including PCA coordinates, per-sample missingness, the PCA outlier flag, chrY mean methylation, and the chrY-based sex-mismatch flag), and the corresponding filtered probe annotation table were saved as R binary (`.rds`) files (`beta_raw.rds`, `pheno.rds`, `anno450k.rds` respectively) for use as input to subsequent analysis stages. Two tables were additionally exported in CSV format for inspection outside R: the phenotype table with QC annotations, and the full PCA/sex-check results table (which additionally retains the median-split sex prediction and the raw k-means cluster assignment per sample, beyond the summary mismatch flag merged into the phenotype table). The final dimensions of the retained beta matrix were logged to confirm successful completion of the pipeline.

## 2.X.8 Results

**Cohort composition.** Parsing the series-matrix header recovered phenotype records for all 689 samples in GSE42861, with no missing sample identifiers. The cohort comprised 354 rheumatoid arthritis (RA) cases and 335 controls, distributed by self-reported sex as summarized in Table 2.X.1. This distribution matches the cohort size and case-control split reported by Liu et al. (2013), confirming that header parsing recovered the intended cohort structure ahead of the larger data block. Self-reported age ranged from 18 to 70 years (median 54 years). Self-reported smoking status spanned never-, current-, ex-, and occasional-smoker categories, with two samples carrying an unresolved value in that field.

**Table 2.X.1.** Cohort composition by disease status and self-reported sex.

| Group | Female | Male | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| RA | 253 | 101 | 354 |
| Control | 239 | 96 | 335 |
| **Total** | **492** | **197** | **689** |

**Matrix construction.** The parsed beta-value matrix comprised 485,577 probes across all 689 samples, consistent with the approximately 485,000 CpG probes present on the Illumina HumanMethylation450 BeadChip. Sample order in the numeric block matched the independently parsed phenotype table exactly, confirming that no reordering occurred between the two parses of the series-matrix file.

**Sample-level quality control.** No missing beta values were present for any sample in the parsed matrix, indicating a fully complete author-submitted data matrix.

Principal component analysis on the 20,000 most variable probes separated the cohort into two distinct clusters along the first principal component (Figure 2.X.1). This separation aligned with self-reported sex rather than disease status, consistent with the well-established tendency of methylation-array data to separate strongly by sex on early principal components (Teschendorff & Relton, 2018). Applying a robust outlier rule (deviation exceeding five median absolute deviations from the median on either principal component, assessed separately within each sex) flagged three samples as technical outliers, all self-reported female controls (Table 2.X.2). No RA samples and no male samples met the outlier criterion. In the corresponding genome-wide beta-value density plot (Figure 2.X.2), the three flagged samples showed a visibly attenuated low-methylation peak and elevated mid-range density relative to the cohort mean, indicating that their outlier status reflects a genuine deviation in global methylation profile rather than an artefact confined to the variance-selected probe subset used for the PCA.

Cross-checking self-reported sex against chromosome-Y methylation signal showed complete concordance. Mean chrY beta values were entirely non-overlapping between the sexes (female range 0.334–0.460; male range 0.513–0.574), and unsupervised clustering of this signal, mapped to sex by majority concordance, agreed with self-reported sex for all 689 samples, with no sample- or label-swaps detected.

**Probe-level filtering.** The sequential filtering cascade is summarized in Table 2.X.3. Restricting to `cg`-prefixed probes removed 3,156 SNP-genotyping and non-CpG probes. Removing probes flagged by the Zhou et al. (2017) MASK_general annotation removed a further 59,901 probes. The subsequent multi-hit filter removed no additional probes, indicating that for this dataset the MASK_general annotation had already fully subsumed the multi-mapping probe list. Removing chromosome X and Y probes excluded a further 10,028 probes. No probes were excluded by the final missingness threshold, consistent with the complete absence of missing values noted above; correspondingly, no residual values required imputation in the final matrix.

**Final dataset.** The quality-controlled, filtered dataset comprised 412,492 probes across 689 samples, representing 85.0% of the initially parsed probe set, and was carried forward to subsequent analysis stages.
